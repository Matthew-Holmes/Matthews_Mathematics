\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 1, Question 1}
\hypertarget{work-st1}{}
\small
\textbf{Question:} On numberline A, what numbers are marked by \(P\) and \(Q\)?

\awork{Solution: Line A goes from \(0\) to \(10\). The distance from one tick to the next is \(1\). Point \(P\) is \(3\) full steps from \(0\), so \(P=3\). Point \(Q\) is \(8\) full steps from \(0\), so \(Q=8\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 1, Question 2}
\small
\textbf{Question:} On numberline B, what numbers are marked by \(S\) and \(T\)?

\awork{Solution: Line B goes from \(0\) to \(1\) in \(10\) equal parts, so each small part is \(0.1\). Point \(S\) is \(3\) parts from \(0\), so \(S=0.3\). Point \(T\) is \(9\) parts from \(0\), so \(T=0.9\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 1, Question 3}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Mark \(6\) on Line A and \(0.45\) on Line B.

\awork{Solution: On Line A, each whole number is one tick apart. Mark the tick that is \(6\) steps from \(0\). On Line B, the ticks count tenths, so \(0.4\) is the fourth tenth and \(0.5\) is the fifth tenth. The number \(0.45\) is halfway between \(0.4\) and \(0.5\), so mark it midway between those two ticks.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 1, Question 4}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Sam says \(P=4\), as it is the \(4\)th mark. What has he done wrong?

\awork{Solution: Sam counted the marks instead of counting the steps from \(0\). The tick at \(0\) is the starting point, not the number \(1\). Point \(P\) is \(3\) whole steps after the \(0\) tick, so its value is \(3\), not \(4\). The value of a point on a number line is how far it is from \(0\), not where it comes in a list of ticks.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 1, Question 5}
\small
\textbf{Question:} The chart shows \(407.52\). What is the \(4\) worth? And the \(5\)?

\awork{Solution: In the number \(407.52\), the \(4\) is in the hundreds column, so it is worth \(400\). The \(5\) is in the tenths column, so it is worth \(0.5\) or \(\frac{5}{10}\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 1, Question 6}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Work out \(407.52\times 10\) and \(407.52\div 10\).

\awork{Solution: Multiplying by \(10\) moves every digit one place to the left, so \(407.52\times 10=4075.2\). Dividing by \(10\) moves every digit one place to the right, so \(407.52\div 10=40.752\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 1, Question 7}
\small
\textbf{Question:} A numberline goes \(3.6\) to \(3.7\) in \(10\) equal steps. What is \(3\) steps along from \(3.6\)?

\awork{Solution: The distance from \(3.6\) to \(3.7\) is \(0.1\). One step is therefore \(\frac{0.1}{10}=0.01\). Three steps are \(3\times 0.01=0.03\). Starting at \(3.6\), add \(0.03\) to get \(3.63\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 1, Question 8}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Use \(7\), \(5\), \(4\), \(2\) and a decimal point to make the largest number less than \(100\).

\awork{Solution: To keep the number less than \(100\), the part before the decimal point must be at most two digits. To make it as large as possible, put the biggest digit, \(7\), in the tens column and the next biggest, \(5\), in the ones column, giving \(75\). Then put the remaining digits in descending order after the decimal point, \(.42\). The largest number is therefore \(75.42\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 2, Question 1}
\hypertarget{work-st2}{}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Fill in \( < \) or \( > \): \(7 \underline{\hspace{0.4em}} 12\), \(30 \underline{\hspace{0.4em}} 3\).

\awork{Solution: \(7\) is smaller than \(12\), so \(7<12\). \(30\) is larger than \(3\), so \(30>3\). The inequality sign always opens towards the larger number.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 2, Question 2}
\small
\textbf{Question:} In \(0.405\), what is the \(4\) worth?

\awork{Solution: In \(0.405\), the digit \(4\) is in the tenths column. It is worth \(4\) tenths, which is \(0.4\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 2, Question 3}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Using the place value chart, which is bigger, \(0.4\) or \(0.35\)? Why?

\awork{Solution: Write \(0.4\) as \(0.40\). Then compare the digits after the decimal point from left to right. Both numbers have \(0\) units. In the tenths column, \(0.4\) has \(4\) tenths, but \(0.35\) has only \(3\) tenths. Since \(4>3\), \(0.4\) is bigger.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 2, Question 4}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Fill in \(<\), \(>\) or \(=\): \(0.4 \underline{\hspace{0.4em}} 0.40\), \(0.6 \underline{\hspace{0.4em}} 0.06\).

\awork{Solution: The trailing zero in \(0.40\) does not change the value, so \(0.4=0.40\). For the second pair, \(0.6\) is \(6\) tenths, while \(0.06\) is only \(6\) hundredths, so \(0.6>0.06\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 2, Question 5}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Mark \(0.38\) on Line A. Is it nearer \(0.3\) or \(0.4\)?

\awork{Solution: Line A goes from \(0.3\) to \(0.4\) in ten equal parts, so each part is \(0.01\). \(0.38\) is eight of these parts after \(0.3\), so it is two parts before \(0.4\). Its distance to \(0.3\) is \(0.08\), and its distance to \(0.4\) is \(0.02\). Hence it is nearer to \(0.4\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 2, Question 6}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Order, smallest first: \(0.5\), \(0.45\), \(0.405\), \(0.54\).

\awork{Solution: Line up the place values by adding zeros: \(0.500\), \(0.450\), \(0.405\), \(0.540\). Compare tenths first: \(0.405\) and \(0.450\) both have \(4\) tenths, but \(0.405\) has \(0\) hundredths and \(0.450\) has \(5\), so \(0.405<0.45\). Then \(0.500<0.540\). Therefore the order is \(0.405\), \(0.45\), \(0.5\), \(0.54\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 2, Question 7}
\small
\textbf{Question:} True or false: more digits after the decimal point means bigger? Give an example.

\awork{Solution: False. A decimal with more digits can still be smaller if its first decimal digit is smaller. For example, \(0.9\) has 1 decimal digit and \(0.1234\) has 4 decimal digits, but \(0.9>0.1234\), because \(0.9\) has \(9\) tenths whereas \(0.1234\) has only \(1\) tenth.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 2, Question 8}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Alfie says \(0.45>0.5\) because \(45>5\). Explain his mistake, then write a decimal between \(0.4\) and \(0.405\).

\awork{Solution: Alfie is comparing \(45\) and \(5\) as if they were whole numbers, ignoring the decimal point. In fact, \(0.45=4\) tenths and \(5\) hundredths, whereas \(0.5=5\) tenths \(=50\) hundredths. Since \(50\) hundredths is greater than \(45\) hundredths, \(0.5>0.45\). A decimal between \(0.4\) and \(0.405\) is \(0.402\), since \(0.4=0.400\) and \(0.400<0.402<0.405\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 3, Question 1}
\hypertarget{work-st3}{}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Which is colder, \(4\,^\circ\)C or \(-4\,^\circ\)C?

\awork{Solution: \(4\,^\circ\)C is above freezing, while \(-4\,^\circ\)C is below freezing. On a number line, \(-4\) lies to the left of \(4\), so \(-4\) is smaller. Therefore \(-4\,^\circ\)C is colder.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 3, Question 2}
\small
\textbf{Question:} What is each mark on the thermometer worth? Write the temperatures at A and B.

\awork{Solution: The thermometer goes from \(-10\) to \(10\), a range of \(20\) degrees, in \(10\) equal intervals. Each mark is therefore \(20\div 10=2\,^\circ\)C. Point A is three \(2\)-degree marks below \(0\), so \(A=-6\,^\circ\)C. Point B is two \(2\)-degree marks above \(0\), so \(B=4\,^\circ\)C.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 3, Question 3}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Which is colder, \(-3\) or \(-8\)? Fill in: \(-3 \underline{\hspace{0.6em}} -8\).

\awork{Solution: On a number line, \(-8\) is further to the left than \(-3\), so \(-8\) is colder and smaller. Therefore the sign must open towards \(-3\): \(-3>-8\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 3, Question 4}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Order, smallest first: \(-2\), \(5\), \(-7\), \(0\), \(-1\).

\awork{Solution: Negative numbers are smaller than \(0\), and among negative numbers the one further left on the number line is smaller. The numbers in increasing order are therefore \(-7\), \(-2\), \(-1\), \(0\), \(5\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 3, Question 5}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Mark \(-0.5\) and \(-0.05\) on Line C. Which is larger?

\awork{Solution: Line C goes from \(-1\) to \(0\) in tenths. \(-0.5\) is halfway between \(-1\) and \(0\). \(-0.05\) is halfway between \(-0.1\) and \(0\), so it is very close to \(0\). Because \(-0.05\) is further to the right on the number line, \(-0.05\) is the larger number.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 3, Question 6}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Fill in \( < \) or \( > \): \(-4.5 \underline{\hspace{0.6em}} -4.05\), \(-0.3 \underline{\hspace{0.6em}} 0.03\).

\awork{Solution: \(-4.5\) is below \(-4.05\) on the number line, so \(-4.5<-4.05\). Also, any negative number is smaller than any positive number, so \(-0.3<0.03\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 3, Question 7}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Order, smallest first: \(-0.08\), \(0.7\), \(-0.8\), \(-0.75\).

\awork{Solution: The positive number \(0.7\) is greatest. Among the negative numbers, \(-0.8\) is further left than \(-0.75\), and both are further left than \(-0.08\). Therefore the order is \(-0.8\), \(-0.75\), \(-0.08\), \(0.7\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 3, Question 8}
\small
\textbf{Question:} List the integers \(n\): \(-3\leq n<2\). Write a number between \(-0.5\) and \(-0.4\).

\awork{Solution: The inequality \(-3\leq n<2\) means that \(n\) can be \(-3\) but cannot be \(2\). So the integers are \(-3,-2,-1,0,1\). A number between \(-0.5\) and \(-0.4\) is, for example, \(-0.45\), because \(-0.5<-0.45<-0.4\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 4, Question 2}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 4, Question 4}

\textbf{Question:} Mark \(3472\) on Line B, then round it to the nearest \(100\).

\awork{Solution: On Line B, the marked interval goes from \(3400\) to \(3500\). Since each small interval is \(10\), \(3472\) is \(72\) after \(3400\). To round to the nearest \(100\), compare the distance to \(3400\), which is \(72\), with the distance to \(3500\), which is \(28\). \begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 4, Question 5}

\awork{Solution: To the nearest \(10\), look at the ones digit of \(3472\), which is \(2<5\), so the tens digit stays as \(7\): \(3470\). To the nearest \(1000\), look at the hundreds digit, which is \(4<5\), so the thousands digit stays as \(3\): \(3000\).}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 4, Question 7}

\textbf{Question:} Zoe rounds \(8449\) to the nearest \(100\): \(8449\to 8450\to 8500\). What is her mistake?

\awork{Solution: Zoe rounds twice. She first rounds \(8449\) to the nearest \(10\), getting \(8450\), and then rounds \(8450\) to the nearest \(100\). \awork{Solution: The integers that round to \(250\) when rounding to the nearest \(10\) are the integers from \(245\) up to \(254\). \(245\) rounds up to \(250\), and \(254\) rounds down to \(250\). So the smallest is \(245\) and the largest is \(254\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 5, Question 1}
\hypertarget{work-st5}{}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Round \(4738\) to the nearest \(100\).

\awork{Solution: To round to the nearest \(100\), look at the tens digit. In \(4738\), the tens digit is \(3<5\), so the hundreds digit stays as \(7\) and the digits after it become zero. Thus \(4738\) rounds to \(4700\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 5, Question 2}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Round to the nearest whole number: \(4.6\), \(12.348\).

\awork{Solution: For \(4.6\), the tenths digit is \(6\ge 5\), so round up to \(5\). For \(12.348\), the tenths digit is \(3<5\), so the whole-number part stays as \(12\). Thus the answers are \(5\) and \(12\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 5, Question 3}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Mark \(2.763\) on Line A. Round it to \(1\) decimal place (d.p.).

\awork{Solution: On Line A, \(2.763\) lies between \(2.76\) and \(2.77\), just past the \(2.76\) position. To round to \(1\) d.p., look at the second decimal digit of \(2.763\), which is \(6\ge 5\), so the first decimal digit \(7\) rounds up to \(8\). Therefore \(2.763\) rounds to \(2.8\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 5, Question 4}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Round to \(1\) d.p.: \(4.62\), \(0.95\). Why is \(0.95\) not \(1\)?

\awork{Solution: For \(4.62\), the second decimal digit is \(2<5\), so \(4.62\) rounds to \(4.6\). For \(0.95\), the second decimal digit is \(5\), so \(0.9\) rounds up to \(1.0\). We write \(1.0\), not \(1\), because giving a number to \(1\) d.p. requires exactly one digit after the decimal point.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 5, Question 5}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Round \(12.348\) to \(2\) d.p., then to \(1\) d.p. Why not \(12.4\)?

\awork{Solution: To \(2\) d.p., look at the third decimal digit \(8\ge 5\), so \(12.34\) becomes \(12.35\). To \(1\) d.p., look at the second decimal digit of the original number: it is \(4<5\), so \(12.348\) rounds to \(12.3\). It is not \(12.4\), because that would come from first rounding to \(12.35\) and then rounding again. Never round in stages.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 5, Question 6}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Line B shows \(\frac{2}{3}=0.\dot{6}\). Round it to \(1\) d.p. and \(2\) d.p.

\awork{Solution: Since \(0.\dot{6}=0.6666\ldots\), to \(1\) d.p.\ look at the hundredths digit \(6\ge 5\), so it rounds up to \(0.7\). To \(2\) d.p., look at the thousandths digit \(6\ge 5\), so it rounds up to \(0.67\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 5, Question 7}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Write \(\frac{1}{3}\) in dot notation, then round \(\frac{1}{7}\) to \(3\) d.p.

\awork{Solution: \(\frac{1}{3}=0.3333\ldots =0.\dot{3}\). Also, \(\frac{1}{7}=0.142857142857\ldots\). To round to \(3\) d.p., look at the fourth decimal digit, which is \(8\ge 5\). Therefore \(\frac{1}{7}\) rounds to \(0.143\).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Solution: Retrieve and Connect 5, Question 8}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Which listed decimals never settle into a repeating block? Can one still be a fraction?

\awork{Solution: Of the decimals listed, \(\pi=3.14159265\ldots\), \(\sqrt{2}=1.41421356\ldots\), and \(0.1010010001\ldots\) never settle into a repeating block. The others, \(0.\dot{6}\) and \(0.142857\ldots\), do repeat. A never-ending decimal can still be a fraction if it eventually repeats, for example \(0.\dot{3}=\frac{1}{3}\). The three non-repeating decimals above cannot be written as fractions.}
